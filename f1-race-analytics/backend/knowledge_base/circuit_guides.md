# Circuit guides

## Austin (Circuit of the Americas)

A purpose-built modern circuit in Texas combining a steep uphill run to Turn 1 (inspired by Silverstone's Maggotts-Becketts) with a long back straight and a flowing, high-speed final sector reminiscent of Istanbul Park. The elevation change at Turn 1 creates a genuine overtaking opportunity into the first corner, and the wide track surface generally supports more side-by-side racing than many modern venues.

## Baku (Baku City Circuit)

A street circuit of contrasts: a very long, flat-out straight along the seafront capable of the highest top speeds of the season, feeding into an extremely tight, slow castle-section through Baku's old city where the track squeezes to barely a car's width past a stone wall. That combination — huge straight-line speed differences plus a demanding, wall-lined technical section — makes it a track where safety cars and red flags are common and strategy can be upended in an instant.

## Catalunya (Circuit de Barcelona-Catalunya)

A long-standing pre-season testing venue, so teams and drivers arrive with an unusually deep well of prior data on this track. A mix of high-speed, sustained-load corners (particularly the final sector) makes it demanding on tyres, especially the front-left, and historically a circuit where aerodynamic efficiency across a full lap — not just outright downforce — separates the quicker cars.

## Hungaroring

A tight, twisty, relatively low-speed circuit often compared to a "go-kart track" for its constant stream of medium- and low-speed corners with few real straights. Overtaking is difficult given the lack of long braking zones, which puts a premium on qualifying well; the circuit also sits in a natural amphitheater-like bowl that traps heat, making it one of the hotter, more physically demanding races of the season.

## Imola (Autodromo Enzo e Dino Ferrari)

A historic, narrow circuit run anticlockwise with very little run-off in places, following the natural contours of the land through fast, blind changes of direction. Its narrowness and lack of long straights make overtaking genuinely difficult, so — like Monaco — track position from qualifying carries outsized importance here.

## Jeddah (Jeddah Corniche Circuit)

One of the fastest street circuits on the calendar: a long, flowing layout along Jeddah's coastline with very high average speeds for a street track, walls close on both sides, and limited visibility through several blind, high-speed corners. That combination of speed and proximity to the walls makes it a circuit where a small mistake carries big consequences, and safety cars are common.

## Kuala Lumpur

A circuit hosting a Grand Prix on the current calendar in Malaysia; specific layout characteristics aren't included in this knowledge base yet. Treat any detailed claims about this circuit's corners or history as unconfirmed until sourced from race data or an authoritative reference.

## Las Vegas (Las Vegas Strip Circuit)

A street circuit built along and around the Las Vegas Strip, run at night, combining very long straights (including a run down Las Vegas Boulevard) with tighter, technical sections around the surrounding city blocks. High top speeds on the straights plus a cold desert-night track surface make tyre warm-up and traction out of the slower corners a notable challenge.

## Lusail (Losail International Circuit)

A flowing, high-speed circuit originally built for motorcycle racing, with a long, sweeping lap of predominantly medium- to high-speed corners and few slow sections. That layout puts sustained load through the tyres for long stretches of the lap, and — being run at night in a desert climate — track temperature can swing significantly through a session.

## Madring

A newer addition to the calendar in Madrid, combining street-circuit sections with parts of a permanent facility. As a recent addition, less historical race data exists for this circuit than for longer-standing venues on the calendar.

## Melbourne (Albert Park)

A semi-street circuit around a public park, historically bumpy but resurfaced and reprofiled in recent years to be faster and more flowing, with several corners now capable of being taken with much less lift than before. Often the season-opening race, meaning it's the first real look at the competitive order after pre-season testing.

## Mexico City (Autódromo Hermanos Rodríguez)

Sits at very high altitude (over 2,200 meters), which thins the air enough to meaningfully reduce both aerodynamic downforce and power unit cooling efficiency — teams run unusually high-downforce wing packages here specifically to compensate for the thin air. The circuit's signature feature is the stadium section, where the track cuts through a baseball stadium's grandstands, generating an especially loud, enclosed atmosphere.

## Miami (Miami International Autodrome)

A street-style circuit built around the Hard Rock Stadium complex, mixing a long straight where DRS and slipstreaming matter with tighter, technical sections around the stadium itself. A relatively new addition to the calendar, run in the heat and humidity of a Florida spring.

## Monaco (Monte Carlo)

A tight, narrow street circuit through the streets of Monte Carlo with almost no run-off area — a single mistake usually means hitting a wall. Overtaking is extremely difficult given the lack of long straights or wide braking zones, which makes qualifying position, and pole position especially, unusually decisive for the race result. Cars run their highest downforce setup of the season here, prioritizing cornering grip over straight-line speed since there's so little straight to speed on.

## Montreal (Circuit Gilles Villeneuve)

A semi-permanent circuit on an island in the Saint Lawrence River, characterized by long straights connected by tight chicanes, making it relatively low-downforce for a track with as many corners as it has. The exit of the final chicane runs right alongside a wall nicknamed the "Wall of Champions" for the number of championship-winning drivers who've hit it there, and the circuit's combination of long braking zones and close walls makes it a common venue for safety cars.

## Monza (Autodromo Nazionale Monza)

Known as the "Temple of Speed" — long straights and only a handful of chicanes mean cars run their lowest-downforce setup of the season to minimize drag. Slipstreaming (drafting behind another car to reduce your own drag) is a major factor in both qualifying and overtaking. Braking zones into the chicanes are extremely heavy given the high approach speeds.

## Sakhir (Bahrain International Circuit)

A permanent desert circuit with a mix of long straights and tight, technical corners, often used for pre-season testing as well as its own Grand Prix, meaning it's usually one of the most heavily-run and well-understood tracks by teams heading into a season. Its abrasive track surface tends to produce higher tyre degradation than many circuits, making pit strategy an unusually large factor here.

## Shanghai (Shanghai International Circuit)

Features an unusual, snail-shell-shaped Turn 1-2-3 complex with a continuously tightening radius, plus one of the longest back straights on the calendar leading into a hard braking zone — a strong overtaking opportunity, especially with DRS. The circuit's size and shape place varied demands on the car across the lap, rewarding a well-rounded setup over one optimized for a single characteristic.

## Silverstone (Silverstone Circuit)

A fast, flowing circuit built around a series of high-speed corners (Copse, and the Maggotts-Becketts-Chapel sequence in particular), which puts sustained heavy lateral load through the tyres — especially the front-left in this configuration — making tyre degradation management a bigger factor here than at many other circuits. Weather is also notably changeable, and rain during the race is common.

## Singapore (Marina Bay Street Circuit)

A bumpy, high-downforce street circuit raced at night under floodlights. Physically demanding due to heat and humidity combined with a long lap full of low-speed corners. Historically one of the highest safety-car-probability races on the calendar, given the unforgiving walls lining a street track — which itself becomes a strategic factor, since a well-timed pit stop just before a safety car is deployed can gain a driver significant track position for free.

## Spa-Francorchamps (Circuit de Spa-Francorchamps)

A long circuit, one of the longest on the calendar, through the Ardennes forest, famous for the Eau Rouge–Raidillon uphill compression and the very long Kemmel straight that follows it. Significant elevation change across the lap and highly localized weather (it can be raining at one part of the circuit and dry at another) make it one of the more unpredictable races strategically.

## Spielberg (Red Bull Ring)

A short, relatively simple lap by modern F1 standards, but with significant elevation change and several long, hard-braking zones into slow corners that create genuine overtaking opportunities. Its short lap length means a very high number of laps for the race distance, and its position in the Austrian mountains makes weather changeable within a single session.

## Suzuka (Suzuka International Racing Course)

A technical, high-speed circuit famous for its figure-eight layout (the track crosses over itself via a bridge) and the flowing "Esses" section early in the lap, which rewards a car with strong high-speed cornering balance. Considered one of the more physically and technically demanding circuits for drivers to get right over a single qualifying lap.

## Yas Marina Circuit

A modern circuit in Abu Dhabi that typically hosts the final race of the season, combining long straights with tighter, harbor-front sections around a marina and hotel. Because it's usually the championship decider, the racing narrative there is often shaped as much by points-table implications as by the track itself; the circuit layout has been reprofiled in recent years to encourage more overtaking than its earlier, more processional configuration allowed.

## Zandvoort (Circuit Zandvoort)

A historic dune circuit on the Dutch coast, reintroduced to the calendar with several steeply banked corners (most notably the final corner) designed to allow cars to carry more speed through them and enable closer racing. Narrow in places and lined by dunes rather than the run-off areas common at more modern circuits, which raises the cost of a mistake.
