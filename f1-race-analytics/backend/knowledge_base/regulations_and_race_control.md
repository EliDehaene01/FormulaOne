# Regulations and race control basics

## Points system

Points are awarded to the top ten finishers in a Grand Prix: 25-18-15-12-10-8-6-4-2-1 for 1st through 10th. An additional point goes to whoever sets the fastest lap of the race, provided they finish in the top ten. On sprint weekends, the top eight finishers in the sprint session earn a separate, smaller points scale (8-7-6-5-4-3-2-1), added on top of the main race's points. Exact numbers can shift between seasons, so treat this as the standard recent format rather than a permanently fixed rule.

## Parc fermé

From the moment qualifying begins until the race starts, cars are held under parc fermé rules: teams cannot make significant setup changes (suspension, aerodynamic components, gearing, and similar). Only a short list of exceptions are permitted — things like tyre pressure adjustments within a set range, or repairs following damage. A team that needs a bigger change than the rules allow can make it, but the car must then start the race from the pit lane rather than its qualified grid position.

## Flags and race control signals

- **Yellow flag** — danger ahead; drivers must slow down and are barred from overtaking in that sector.
- **Double yellow** — more severe danger; be prepared to stop if necessary.
- **Blue flag** — shown to a driver about to be lapped by a faster car, signaling them to let it pass.
- **Red flag** — the session is stopped entirely, usually for a serious incident or unsafe conditions.
- **Checkered flag** — the session or race has ended.

## Safety car vs virtual safety car

A **safety car (SC)** is a real car deployed onto the circuit; the entire field bunches up behind it at a controlled pace until the incident is cleared, at which point it peels into the pits and racing resumes. A **virtual safety car (VSC)** achieves a similar slowdown without a physical car — every driver instead must stay under a specified delta time (calculated per sector) until it's lifted. VSCs are typically used for less severe incidents than a full safety car, and are quicker to deploy and end.

## Qualifying format

A standard qualifying session runs in three knockout stages:

- **Q1** (typically around 18 minutes): all cars run; the slowest few are eliminated and start the race from the back in that order.
- **Q2** (around 15 minutes): the remaining cars run again; another group is eliminated.
- **Q3** (around 12 minutes): the fastest remaining cars fight for pole position.

Whatever tyre compound a driver used to set their fastest Q2 time typically becomes their mandatory starting compound for the race, with some exceptions (such as drivers who didn't progress past Q1).

## Grid penalties

Penalties can move a driver back a fixed number of grid positions, most commonly for causing a collision, ignoring race control instructions, or exceeding the number of power unit components allowed per season without penalty. If a driver's total penalties exceed the number of grid positions available, they're moved to the back of the grid instead, ordered relative to other penalized drivers.
