# Tyre compounds and strategy

## Compound naming

Pirelli produces tyre compounds numbered C1 (hardest) through C5 (softest) across a season. For each race weekend, Pirelli selects three of these five compounds and labels them, for that weekend only, as hard, medium, and soft. This means "the hard tyre" at one circuit might be a genuinely different compound than "the hard tyre" at another race — the labels are relative to that weekend's selection, not a fixed compound identity.

There are also two wet-weather tyres: intermediates (for damp or drying tracks) and full wets (for standing water), both visually distinguished by grooved tread patterns rather than the smooth surface of dry-weather tyres.

## Why softer isn't always faster

A softer compound generates more grip and a faster single lap, but wears out and overheats faster — it degrades sooner in a stint. A harder compound is slower on a single lap but holds its performance over a longer stint. Race strategy is largely a trade-off between these two: pit more often on faster, shorter-lived tyres, or pit less on slower, longer-lived ones.

## Degradation mechanisms

- **Wear** is physical: the rubber compound literally grinds away against the track surface over a stint.
- **Thermal degradation** is chemical: tyres have an optimal operating temperature window, and running above it breaks down the rubber's internal structure, permanently reducing grip even after the tyre cools back down.
- **Graining** is a surface effect where the tyre's outer layer shears and rolls rather than gripping cleanly, usually early in a stint when tyres are still cool. It often improves as the tyre warms up rather than being a permanent loss.
- **Blistering** is small gas pockets forming under the tread surface from excess heat — a more serious, largely irreversible loss of performance for the rest of that stint.

## Strategy concepts

**Undercut** — pitting before a rival. The driver who pits first loses track position temporarily (they fall behind while circulating through the pits) but gains it back once their fresh, faster tyres let them out-pace the rival still on old tyres — ideally emerging ahead once the rival eventually pits too.

**Overcut** — the reverse: staying out longer than a rival. This works when track position is worth more than tyre freshness at that moment, often because the pit lane itself costs significant time, or because a driver is stuck in traffic where clean air matters more than outright tyre pace.

**Tyre offset strategy** — deliberately starting or pitting onto a different compound than the cars around you, betting that a different degradation curve will pay off later in the stint even if it's not optimal at that exact moment.

**Two-stop vs one-stop race** — the number of pit stops in a race is itself a strategic choice: fewer stops save the roughly 20-25 seconds typically lost per pit visit, but usually mean managing more tyre degradation over longer stints; more stops keep tyres fresher but add up cumulative pit-lane time.
